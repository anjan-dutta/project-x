System design / raw materials procurement

Procurement Timing Engine

Tells a factory which raw material to order, how much, and by what date, given its own consumption history, its suppliers' actual reliability, and what is happening in the world.

The premise

"When should I buy" is not a prediction. It is a decision that falls out of two predictions plus a policy. Modelling the purchase date directly is the common failure, because the date is not a property of the world, it is the output of the policy you choose.

So the system forecasts consumption and lead time as distributions, then lets inventory theory convert them into a reorder decision. Global events do not enter as model features. They shift the parameters of those two distributions.

How it fits together

The four layers

L1

Demand, as a distribution

Raw material demand is derived, not independent. Inside the MRP horizon you do not forecast at all: you explode the production plan through the bill of materials and adjust for scrap yield. Beyond it, you forecast.

Segment first by ABC value and demand pattern (ADI against CV²). statsforecast ETS/ARIMA for smooth series, LightGBM with quantile loss where covariates matter, Croston / TSB for intermittent items. That last case is not an edge case: many raw materials are ordered a handful of times a year, and ARIMA on a mostly-zero series produces confident nonsense.

L2

Lead time, with its variance

Fit a distribution per supplier and material from promised-versus-actual history; log-normal fits observed lead times well. Lead time variability usually dominates demand variability here. A supplier averaging 30 days ±12 needs far more buffer than one averaging 40 days ±2.

L3

The policy that produces a date

Reorder point and safety stock give the closed form. For A-class items, replace the division with a Monte Carlo that samples both distributions forward and finds the latest order date holding stockout probability under target. Simulation handles lumpy demand and price breaks the formula cannot.

L4

The event overlay

Claude reads global signals into a fixed schema, deduplicates across the hundreds of articles one event generates, and scores mechanism, magnitude, onset and duration. A hand-authored transmission map turns that into parameter deltas: port disruption at Rotterdam → lanes through Rotterdam → lead time +12d, σ +8d, decaying over 45 days.

Hand-authored, because you cannot train this. Major disruptions are rare, so you have perhaps ten to twenty per commodity across all history. That is an anecdote collection, not a training set.

The decision math

// reorder point ROP = (avg daily demand × lead time) + safety stock // safety stock, both variances SS = z × √( LT·σ²_demand + demand²·σ²_LT ) // the answer the user actually sees days_to_order = (inventory_position − ROP) / forecast_daily_use inventory_position = on_hand + on_order − allocated − backorders

The second term inside the root is the one people drop. Without it you buffer against demand noise and get blindsided by a supplier who is merely inconsistent.

Parameters that carry weight

GroupWhat matters
Demand Bill of materials and production plan (the highest-value input you can obtain), open orders and backlog, consumption history, scrap and yield rate, seasonality, lifecycle stage
Supply Lead time mean and σ per supplier-material, on-time delivery rate, MOQ and lot increments, supplier capacity, single versus multi-source, customs and port dwell for imports
Inventory On-hand, in-transit with ETAs, allocated, shelf life and expiry, storage capacity and special handling
Economics Holding cost at 15–30% of unit value per year, ordering cost per PO, price-break tiers, working capital ceiling, and line-down cost per hour
External Commodity indices, price volatility and futures curve, FX on imported inputs, freight and container rates, tariffs and export controls, sector PMI

Stockout cost typically runs 10–100× holding cost. That asymmetry is why the optimal policy deliberately over-stocks relative to a naive cost-minimiser. Encode it explicitly or the model will look accurate and lose the customer money.

Build sequence

Each step depends on the one before it. Step 1 alone beats the spreadsheet-and-instinct process most mid-size factories run on, and it is the fastest route to something demonstrable.

01
ABC/XYZ segmentation plus ROP with safety stock. Computed from historical consumption. No machine learning at all.
02
Probabilistic demand forecasts and fitted lead-time distributions. Per segment, with the model chosen by demand pattern.
03
BOM and production-plan integration. Converts near-horizon guessing into arithmetic.
04
Event overlay and commodity signals. Strategic buy-forward timing on top of a system that already works without it.

What kills this

Cold start
A new factory has no history in your system, so the model is worthless on day one. Onboarding has to ingest their existing ERP or Tally consumption data, and you need a defensible rules-based fallback for the first months. Solve this before the modelling, because it decides whether anyone ever sees the modelling.
Leakage
The event store must be append-only and queryable as of any past timestamp. Backtests may use only what was published before t. Look-ahead leakage is trivially easy to introduce here and produces a spectacular backtest followed by a worthless product.
False alarms
Optimise for precision, not recall. A missed disruption costs a line-down, but a false alarm costs working capital and trust. Cry wolf three times and the feature gets switched off. Ship narrow and correct.
Wrong metric
Judge on service level, fill rate, stockout days, inventory turns and total cost, never forecast accuracy alone. For the forecast itself use MASE or pinball loss. MAPE is undefined on zero-demand periods, so it breaks precisely on the intermittent items that matter most.
The moat
Multi-tier supplier mapping and the decision log. Your customer knows their suppliers but not their suppliers' suppliers, and tier-2 exposure is where disruptions originate. Log every recommendation and outcome from day one: it is your calibration set, and in eighteen months nobody can retroactively acquire it.

Stack

Forecasting
Python service (FastAPI) with statsforecast and LightGBM, plus a nightly job writing forecasts to a collection the BFF reads. Node has no serious forecasting ecosystem, so this stays a separate process.
claude-opus-5
Event extraction and impact reasoning, with structured outputs for the schema and strict: true on tools. Adaptive thinking; effort tuned per route.
Message Batches
Nightly bulk article scoring at 50% cost. The workload is not latency-sensitive, so this fits exactly.
Prompt caching
On the taxonomy, schema and system prompt, which are byte-identical across every article.
Web search
On-demand investigation of a named supplier or commodity when an analyst drills in, domain-restricted.
Event store
Append-only, timestamped, point-in-time queryable. Embeddings and a vector store for deduplication and analogue retrieval.
Voice reporting
Web Speech API in the browser into a tool-use route on the existing Express BFF. Tools wrap existing controllers; the model never emits queries.
